KinTrace

Your family tree, checked against clinical guidelines
AI x Healthcare
Privacy-first web app, runs in the browser

Problem

Family history is the cheapest screening input medicine has, and one of the most under-used. It costs nothing to collect, yet appointments rarely leave time to take it properly.

Most people do not know which relatives matter: that a mother's breast cancer at 38 or a father's heart attack at 57 is exactly what a referral guideline asks about, or that an aunt's ovarian cancer counts on the same side of the family.

Solution

  • Draw the tree, or describe it in a sentence and confirm the suggested entries.
  • Pick a guideline: NICE CG164 (breast and ovarian cancer), Amsterdam II (Lynch syndrome), NICE CG71, the DLCN family-history item and NICE NG238 (early heart disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia).
  • See which criteria are met: Meets criteria Worth discussing No criteria met, each with its rule steps and the relatives who triggered it ringed on the tree.
  • Take a one-page doctor report to the appointment.

Everything except the optional parse function runs in the browser. Any failure of the AI path falls back to the offline parser.

Where AI is used, and why only there

Exactly one place: turning a typed sentence ("My mum had breast cancer at 38") into proposed tree entries. Language is messy; that is where a model helps.

Findings are a different job. A referral criterion is a precise rule with ages and degrees of relationship, so it is code that gives the same answer every time and is tested at each threshold. No model decides, ranks or words a finding. The AI switch is off by default, every proposed entry must be accepted by the user, any entry quoting text that is not in the input is dropped, and an offline parser does the job with no key at all.

Safety and ethics

  • No diagnosis, no percentages. Never a risk figure, probability or score; only which published criteria are met.
  • Unknown is never healthy. A missing age leaves a criterion open (Worth discussing), and a completeness meter shows what is not known.
  • Data stays on the device. No accounts, no database, no analytics. A browser test asserts zero requests to other origins; with AI on, only the typed text goes to the same-origin parse function, which logs no content.
  • Sources shown. Every finding names its guideline, rule steps and review date.

Validation

999unit, component, property and fuzz tests
266browser tests across Chromium, Firefox, WebKit and five widths
78.4%mutation score on the guideline rules
15bugs found by testing, each fixed with a test

Every browser test runs under a guard that fails it on console errors, failed requests, overflow from 320 px wide, accessibility violations or layout shift. The edge tests found, among others: two browser tabs silently overwriting each other's tree, blocked storage reported as saved, a double click adding two people, the report dated the wrong day in some time zones, an adopted user told to "add your parents", and a hung AI service leaving the user waiting with no way to cancel.

Limitations

  • Not a medical device; it does not replace a clinician or genetic counsellor.
  • Guidelines are UK, Dutch and international; local guidance may differ.
  • Spanish and Hindi need a fluent review; the offline parser is English only.
  • Relatives up to third degree; some specialist criteria are not implemented.

Roadmap

  • Other conditions: familial colorectal cancer beyond Lynch, inherited cardiomyopathy and sudden cardiac death.
  • More guidelines, so users can pick the guidance their own health system uses.
  • More languages, each reviewed by fluent speakers, with an offline parser for each.
